The process

How a print is made

Cyanotype is one of the oldest photographic processes there is. It needs iron salts, sunlight and water. No darkroom, no ink.

Coating
01

Coat

Two iron compounds are mixed under dim light and brushed onto washed cotton or linen. Wet, it is a pale chartreuse. It dries in the dark overnight.

02

Arrange

Ferns, grasses, Queen Anne's lace, whatever the season gives. Each stem is laid by hand, pressed flat under glass so the shadow stays crisp.

03

Expose

Out into the sun for eight to twenty minutes depending on the sky. The coating darkens to a bronze grey wherever the light reaches it.

04

Rinse

A long bath in cool water washes away the unexposed chemistry. The blue blooms in the first minute and keeps deepening for a day.

From the studio

Why nothing here is made twice

A cyanotype is a shadow. The plant sits on the cloth, the sun does the rest, and when the water runs clear the shape left behind is exactly that stem on that afternoon. Even if I wanted to, I could not print it again.

So the shop is small on purpose. I list a handful of pieces at a time, usually on a Saturday morning, and when one finds its person it stays on the site marked sold, because I like being able to look back at the whole body of work.

Beth

Wash cold, inside out

Mild soap, no bleach or phosphates. The blue actually deepens over the first few washes.

Dry in the shade

Line dry out of direct sun. Prussian blue is lightfast, but years of noon sun will soften any dye.

Expect character

Brush marks, a faint halo around a leaf, a pinhole of white. Those are the print, not flaws in it.
